## Before We Begin

Even now, I still do not think that succeeding in postgraduate recommendation is an achievement worth wearing like a badge of honor. Everyone has the right to choose their own life and decide how they want to spend their university years, so I do not want this post to be placed on a pedestal as some definitive guide. Nor do I believe I am qualified to preach or present these thoughts as unquestionable rules. If anything here happens to help you, that would already make me very happy.

## What You Need to Know

### The Jargon

Certain terms are used frequently in the postgraduate recommendation process. I use them throughout this article out of habit, so it is worth explaining them first.

- **rk:** Short for “rank.” It refers to your academic ranking. Because universities calculate rankings differently, it may mean either your ranking based solely on grades or your ranking under a broader comprehensive assessment. In most contexts, it means the former.
- **Reaching out:** Often called _taoci_ in Chinese. It means contacting a professor by email before formally applying. If things go well and you pass the professor's evaluation, their support can give you a substantial advantage when applying to that university.
- **Camp admission/outstanding camper:** Universities often organize summer camps, while some hold similar programs as early as spring or winter. Being selected by a department to attend is called “entering the camp.” Participants who perform well may be designated “outstanding campers” by the university.
- **Strong com/weak com:** At a strong-com school, a committee selects students primarily according to hard criteria such as the prestige of their undergraduate institution and their ranking. At a weak-com school, individual supervisors have more influence and the hard requirements are less rigid. Reaching out to professors is highly recommended at weak-com schools because a professor's endorsement can improve your chance of being admitted to the camp.
- **Pre-admission round:** The main admissions round at most universities after the summer camps. Unlike a summer camp, it usually omits campus tours and information sessions. The process moves quickly and focuses mainly on interviews.
- **Casting a wide net:** The Chinese term _hai_, literally “sea,” describes doing something at scale. A student applying to a large number of universities is “casting a wide net,” while a university admitting an excessive number of students to a camp and handing out many ineffective outstanding-camper offers is running a “mass camp.”
- **Reneging:** Sometimes described as “standing someone up” or “being a pigeon” in Chinese. It refers either to a student backing out after accepting a professor's offer or to a professor breaking a commitment made to a student. Reneging is deeply dishonest. Please avoid doing it, and release an offer promptly if you no longer intend to accept it.

### The Process

Recommendation-based postgraduate admission, commonly known in Chinese as _baoyan_, is a path organized by the Ministry of Education through which students can enter postgraduate programs without taking the national entrance examination. It often allows you to choose a university, research direction, and supervisor that suit you better. There are drawbacks as well. In my view, the current selection system can be destructive to undergraduate life and greatly diminish its richness.

Since this is a formal admissions path administered by the Ministry of Education, it follows a defined process. If you choose this route, there are several milestones to keep in mind. Taking my own 2026 application cycle as an example:

## Before Making a Choice

### Competitiveness

People often say that postgraduate recommendation is becoming increasingly competitive, and that is inevitable. The rapid development of AI in recent years, especially advanced agents, has made learning and research far more convenient. As a result, the cost of learning how to implement a research idea—and of actually implementing it—has fallen dramatically for undergraduates. Many applicants now have excellent research outcomes, and a very small number are already experienced researchers by the time they apply.

In today's competition, the three most important factors are clearly rank, competitions, and research. Based on my observations and experience, their current importance is roughly rank $\approx$ research $>$ competitions. Once you understand this balance, you can decide where to focus during your first three undergraduate years. Improving your rank means devoting yourself to your major courses and earning consistently high grades so that you can lead in GPA. Improving your research profile means joining a professor's group and working toward meaningful results. As for competitions, most universities have strong student-run laboratories with supportive cultures, and participating in them can significantly improve your abilities.

It is important to understand that these three factors play different roles. Rank is critical during a university's initial screening, and being the widely recognized rank-one student can give you many privileges.

After passing the initial screening, you will inevitably face interviews designed to test your actual ability. At this point, research becomes much more important. A strong project gives you plenty to discuss, and many professors now place great weight on a student's research potential. A paper alone does not prove that you have strong potential. However, if you completed it systematically during your undergraduate years—independently or as a principal contributor—the research training behind it will give you a much deeper understanding of your field. Your language and answers during interviews will consequently be more mature, displaying the kind of confidence that comes from genuine knowledge and convincing professors that you have the potential to become a good researcher.

Not every discipline allows an undergraduate to produce a strong paper, and competitions can serve as a partial substitute in those fields. Some universities assign scores to competition results and combine them with rank and other factors to decide who passes the initial screening. A competition entry can also be presented as a project during an interview, demonstrating your engineering ability. I deeply admire people who perform well in competitions. To me, competitions demand excellent judgment and the ability to respond under pressure, and students who excel in them must possess exceptional problem-solving skills.

### Joining a Research Group

At some universities, whether you have published a paper carries enormous weight during initial screening. Today, it can even seem as though every applicant has one. Many students therefore join research groups in their first or second undergraduate year, hoping to have strong results by the time they apply.

In theory, the more research outcomes you have—and the more prestigious they are—the better. Inevitably, however, this encourages people to chase papers for their own sake. I have no intention of debating whether that is right or reasonable, since I am not an experienced researcher myself. Everyone makes different choices, and I do not believe pragmatism is shameful. You can choose to be a purist and pursue meaningful research, which usually demands a great deal of time, or accumulate enough papers early and enter industry to put your talents to work. I have always believed that the best choice is the one that suits you.

Joining a research group generally involves several stages. At first, a professor may ask you to study some foundational material, though they may also put you to work immediately. As a novice researcher, you usually cannot lead a project independently, so the professor will assign a master's or doctoral student to mentor you. Good communication with your mentor is the most important part of this stage. I have always believed that healthy communication is essential to moving a project forward. For guidance on communicating effectively, see [How To Ask Questions The Smart Way](https://github.com/ryanhanwu/How-To-Ask-Questions-The-Smart-Way).

If things go smoothly, a period of healthy collaboration may produce a paper on which you are a co-author, marking the result of your initial accumulation of experience. You can then choose to lead a new project independently or continue collaborating with others. As you progress, you will grow and broaden your experience. Positive opportunities may follow: perhaps a professor recognizes your ability and introduces you to more collaborators, or several high-quality papers give you a major advantage during postgraduate recommendation.

Of course, very few people move smoothly through every stage. Many stumble at the first step because research is not an activity in which more effort necessarily guarantees a greater reward. Often, we need to learn to accept the outcome, then either persist or begin again on a different path.

## How to Make Your Choice

### Whether to Pursue a PhD

The mainstream postgraduate paths in China can broadly be divided into academic master's programs, professional master's programs, and direct PhD programs. At most universities, the admissions difficulty is academic master's $>$ professional master's $\approx$ direct PhD. A direct PhD is officially designed to take five years and leads directly to a doctoral degree. In practice, delays of one or two years are not uncommon for reasons beyond the student's control. The resulting duration can be almost the same as completing a master's degree and then a conventional PhD—roughly seven years—while sacrificing both the opportunity to intern in industry earlier and the freedom to decide later whether pursuing a doctorate is truly suitable. Direct PhD programs have consequently received widespread criticism in recent years.

Negative attitudes toward direct PhD programs are common. Yet some universities, especially Tsinghua and Peking University, have sharply reduced places in academic master's programs, leaving applicants to choose between expensive professional master's programs and lengthy direct PhDs. Much of the negativity comes from the sense that master's graduates currently have stronger employment prospects than PhD graduates. After a master's degree, students can pursue industry internships, which have become almost indispensable for securing a well-paid position. Many people also discover only after beginning a direct PhD that they are not suited to conducting research in an environment with few constraints and little guidance.

Direct PhD programs do have advantages. Most obviously, they offer the possibility of earning a doctorate in five years. I also see those years as a relatively pure period in which to conduct research. You can use university resources to do things that may no longer be possible after entering full-time employment, such as engaging in deep academic exchanges with peers in your field. If you have genuine academic passion and a thorough understanding of the field you want to pursue, a direct PhD can be an excellent choice.

Another important consideration is whether you received complete and systematic research training as an undergraduate. Research that you will pursue over many years requires an overall understanding of how the work is done. My undergraduate mentor was an exceptionally experienced and hardworking researcher. Working with my mentor taught me for the first time that research has methodologies that can be followed, and that careful planning can keep progress organized and steady. I also believe that research requires tremendous enthusiasm because many problems cannot be anticipated, and you need enough patience to work through each one even when the solution may ultimately prove useless. Finally, although today's academic environment is far from ideal, novice researchers should at least aspire to produce high-quality papers. Feeling pessimistic about research will not, by itself, help you make progress.

### Choosing a University, Field, and Supervisor

This resembles choosing a university and major after the _gaokao_. Most of the time, university, field, and supervisor form an impossible triangle; only a small number of exceptional students can find an option that is excellent in all three dimensions. You will encounter many opinions about how to prioritize them. In my view, it depends: you need to choose according to your own circumstances.

Whether the _university_ matters is beyond question. A strong university can offer immeasurable resources, but location is equally important. In my own field of integrated circuits, Beijing, Shanghai, and Shenzhen contain nearly all of China's best industrial resources. Attending a university in one of these cities therefore brings enormous advantages for internships and employment. In some fields, a prestigious title is almost equivalent to access to a strong specialization, making the choice of a well-known university especially important.

Your _field_ requires a personal trade-off. In my view, there is no absolutely good or bad field, although people now like to describe them as “Apple fields” and “Android fields”—the favored and unfavored options. If you intend to pursue research, choosing a subject that genuinely interests you matters more than these labels because sustained research depends on strong intrinsic motivation. Of course, not everyone aspires to a research career. That is perfectly normal and not something that deserves criticism; entering industry early is also an excellent choice. If your goal is a generous salary, however, accurately judging which fields are currently on the rise becomes crucial. In some areas, entering early matters more than anything else.

Your _supervisor_ will largely determine how happy your postgraduate life is and can also provide concrete academic and employment resources. Supervisors are often classified in many ways: exploitative or supportive, highly demanding or hands-off, heavily involved in industry projects or focused more narrowly on academic work. Before applying any of these labels, you first need to understand the relationship between yourself, your supervisor, and the research group. Beyond the teacher–student relationship, graduate study involves a degree of mutual exchange. A research group needs students to work on industry-funded projects that cover its operating costs and to publish papers that support the supervisor's career advancement, which in turn brings more resources to the group. Students, meanwhile, gain access to the supervisor's academic and industrial networks. With faculty hiring as competitive as it is today, most newly appointed professors have extensive connections in both academia and industry, which can provide unexpected opportunities whether you seek employment or hope to remain at a university.

Once you understand this logic, you can establish your own priorities when choosing a supervisor. Start with your own working habits. Whether you prefer working spontaneously or following a fixed routine, for example, determines whether you can adapt to a group that requires daily attendance. Your tolerance for pressure determines whether you can keep pace with a highly competitive group. If you choose a PhD, the supervisor's character and personality will also play an enormous role in your well-being over such a long period. Their resources in academia and industry are often a better indicator of a strong group than its reputation alone. I have always believed that you should not blindly pursue a supposedly elite group: the option that suits you is the best one. Admittedly, this advice is somewhat idealistic. Not everyone has time to contact a professor early and learn what the supervisor and group are really like. Sometimes you simply need to let it go. I hope everyone finds a supervisor they genuinely want to work with.

## Facing Life

It is unhealthy to treat academic credentials as the entirety of one's life. The widely circulated image of the “small-town swot” on the Chinese internet is a product of precisely this mindset. I have always believed that we are human beings first and researchers second. By this stage, most students are mature adults. A mature person needs to learn to make decisions about their own future while retaining a love of and confidence in life throughout the process.

After the initial joy has passed, we still need to face life calmly: spend time with family, partners, and friends, and accept praise and love with composure. If the past three years have left you exhausted, the so-called gap year after securing postgraduate admission is an excellent opportunity to return to a normal rhythm of life, study the things you truly love, and cultivate a calm state of mind for the future. Most importantly, develop a sense that you deserve good things and believe that what you have earned belongs naturally in your life.

## About Me

It is difficult for me to offer much unique advice from personal experience because my own application experience was almost nonexistent. After extensive contact during the earlier stages, I applied to only one professor during this admissions season, and I was fortunate that he chose me as well. Judged purely by the outcome, this was undoubtedly risky. The internet offers countless strategies for negotiating among multiple universities and professors to maximize your own benefit, but clearly I used almost none of them.

I am also ashamed to admit that I did not remain calm throughout the process. I care deeply about relationships. When communicating with professors and senior students, I tried to interact in the way I considered ideal. At least in interpersonal matters, I am deeply idealistic and hope to maintain sincere relationships with others. Most of my anxiety came from not knowing whether the professor would treat me with the same sincerity. Fortunately, he is someone who genuinely respects students and treats them honestly. I am once again grateful that my professor placed such complete trust in me.

I have always considered myself extraordinarily fortunate. I have received help and kindness from so many people. My mentor patiently guided me into research, my professor supported me wholeheartedly, and I have friends and classmates whom I can trust completely. Their kindness often makes me feel undeserving and wonder whether I am truly worthy of everything I have received.

I hope that one day I will reach a state in which I believe I deserve what I have earned; that my sincerity can bring true friends into my life; that effort, rather than shortcuts built on manufactured anxiety, can lead me to what I want; and that I can naturally find a compatible partner who is equally kind and has the same faith in herself and in the world. I truly, deeply hope that day will come.
